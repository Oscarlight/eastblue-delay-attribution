\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Advertising systems predict several types of conversion at once, and those types differ sharply
in how long they take to observe. A website view or an add-to-cart is recorded within minutes of
the click; a purchase, an app install or a submitted lead may take a day or a week. Each
objective is defined with an \emph{attribution window}, the elapsed time within which a
conversion is credited to the click. Conventionally that window also determines the \emph{join
delay}, the age at which the click's training record is written to the stream: the pipeline
waits until the label is knowable, then emits the record. This convention has two disadvantages.
First, a model for a slow objective trains only on data at least an attribution window old, so
it never sees fresh data during online training. Second, a system that ranks several objectives
jointly must either maintain one pipeline per objective or delay all of them until the slowest
window closes, imposing the staleness of the slowest funnel on the fastest.

Neither disadvantage is intrinsic to conversion rate modelling. While the attribution window is
fixed by the business definition of a conversion, the join delay is a pipeline choice. We ask
what happens when the two are set independently, and specifically whether an objective with an
attribution window of days can be trained from records joined within minutes.

The obstacle is that an early-joined record is wrong: every conversion still in flight when the
record is written is recorded as a negative. A substantial literature corrects this censoring
bias~\cite{chapelle2014modeling,yasui2020feedback,yang2021capturing,chen2022asymptotically,ding2026delayed},
but most corrections revise the record afterwards, by duplicating the click when its outcome
lands, by relabelling it, or by adding prediction heads at future horizons. A streaming pipeline
that appends each record once and trains on it once can do none of these. We state that
restriction as a \emph{single-visit} constraint.

Our idea is that this censoring bias can be mitigated by the prediction of a model that has been
trained on the matured outcomes in the conventional way, that is, a \emph{teacher model}. Such a
teacher is often readily available in production, where large ranking models are already trained
across objectives on matured data~\cite{meta2025gem}. The contributions of this work are: (1) a
separation of the join delay from the attribution window, together with the causality and
single-visit constraints that a deployable pipeline must satisfy, and an audit of \nScreened{}
published methods finding that \nFail{} of them violate single-visit by writing more than one
record per click; (2) evidence that writing the record early and replacing only its observed
negatives with teacher model predictions beats waiting for the matured label; and (3) the
finding that at small served capacities the hard label can be dropped altogether, since a
training pipeline that uses only the teacher's estimates as labels can beat a conventional
pipeline whose join delay equals the attribution window, which benefits early ranking stages
such as retrieval, where model complexity is low.
